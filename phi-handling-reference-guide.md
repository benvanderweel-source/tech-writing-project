# PHI Handling Reference Guide for Frontline Non-Clinical Healthcare Staff

## Purpose

This guide provides practical HIPAA and Protected Health Information (PHI) handling expectations for frontline non-clinical healthcare staff. It is intended to support safe, appropriate, and professional handling of patient information during daily operations in accordance with federal regulations (45 CFR Parts 160 and 164).

## Who This Applies To

This guide applies to non-clinical staff who interact with patient data, including:

- Receptionists
- Schedulers
- Call center staff
- Registration/intake personnel
- Referral coordinators
- Billing and insurance staff
- Financial counselors
- Medical records staff
- Administrative support staff

## What Is PHI?

Protected Health Information (PHI) is individually identifiable information created, received, or maintained by a covered entity that relates to a patient's:

- Past, present, or future physical or mental health condition
- Provision of healthcare services
- Payment for healthcare services

### Media Formats

PHI may exist in any form or medium, including:

- Electronic records (ePHI)
- Paper documents
- Emails/texts
- Phone conversations
- Voicemails
- Verbal discussions

### Examples of Direct and Indirect Identifiers

- Full name
- Date of birth
- Street address, email address, or phone number
- Social Security Number (SSN) or Medical Record Number (MRN)
- Appointment details (dates, times, provider names)
- Insurance or billing details
- Diagnoses, clinical notes, or test results

## Core Rule: Minimum Necessary Standard

**Rule (45 CFR § 164.502(b) & § 164.514(d)):**

> Only access, use, or disclose the minimum amount of PHI necessary to accomplish your specific, assigned job duties.

### Role-Based Access Examples

- **Schedulers:** Access appointment schedules, provider availability, and basic demographics.
- **Billing Representatives:** Access billing codes, insurance details, and visit dates.
- **Frontline Staff:** Should not access clinical notes or full medical charts unless explicitly required for their administrative role.

### Prohibited Access (Zero Tolerance)

**Never access, view, or look up patient records:**

- Out of personal curiosity
- For personal reasons or family/friends
- For coworkers unless necessary for a shared work assignment
- For public figures, celebrities, or news-related individuals without a direct business need

## Identity Verification

Before disclosing PHI to any caller or visitor, staff must verify the requester's identity and legal authority to receive the information (45 CFR § 164.514(h)).

### Approved Verification Protocols

1. **Prompt the Requester:** Ask the caller/visitor to state at least two to three approved identifiers (e.g., full legal name, date of birth, mailing address, or account/MRN number).
2. **Verify Authority:** Confirm that the requester is the patient or an authorized representative on file (e.g., designated HIPAA disclosure authorization, legal guardian).

### Critical Rules

**Always:**

- Prompt the caller to state identifiers themselves, rather than reading information aloud for them to confirm.
- Complete identity and authority verification before sharing any PHI.

**Never:**

- Read patient information aloud for a caller to confirm.
- Assume a caller is authorized simply because they sound familiar, state they are a relative, or claim an urgent need.

> WRONG: "Is your date of birth 01/01/1980?"
> RIGHT: "Please state your date of birth."

## Phone and Voicemail Guidelines

HIPAA permits leaving voicemails and phone messages, provided reasonable safeguards and minimum necessary limits are applied.

### Leaving Voicemails

**Always:**

- Disclose only the minimum necessary information required for a callback.
- Include specific appointment dates or times only if organizational policy allows and the patient has consented to routine voicemail reminders.

**Never:**

- Leave clinical diagnoses, test results, sensitive treatment details, full birth dates, or Social Security Numbers on a voicemail message.

**Appropriate Voicemail Example:**

> "Hello, this is ABC Clinic calling for Jane Doe. Please return our call at 555-0199 regarding your upcoming appointment. Thank you."

### In-Person & Active Phone Calls

**Always:**

- Speak in lowered tones in areas where others might hear.
- Confirm recipient phone numbers and extension transfers before sending calls through.

**Never:**

- Discuss PHI near waiting rooms, corridors, or public check-in desks.

## Workstation and Screen Security

Physical and technical safeguards protect electronic PHI (ePHI) from unauthorized viewing (45 CFR § 164.310 & § 164.312).

**Always:**

- Lock your computer screen (Win + L or system lock) whenever stepping away, even for a moment.
- Position monitors away from public view or install privacy screen filters.
- Keep physical charts and paperwork covered or face down.
- Log into applications using only your assigned credentials.

**Never:**

- Share user passwords, authentication tokens, or badges.
- Write passwords on sticky notes, desks, or reference sheets.
- Leave patient charts or printed schedules unattended.
- Use personal unauthorized devices, personal cloud storage, or personal email for work tasks.

## Email, Texting, and Messaging

Use only organization-approved, encrypted communication channels to transmit PHI.

**Always:**

- Verify the recipient's exact email address or fax number before sending.
- Double-check attached files to ensure they match the intended recipient.
- Encrypt external emails containing PHI per organizational software guidelines (e.g., using [SECURE] in the subject line).

**Never:**

- Use personal email accounts (Gmail, Yahoo, etc.) to conduct healthcare business.
- Send PHI via unapproved third-party messaging apps (SMS, iMessage, WhatsApp).
- Forward work emails containing PHI to personal accounts.

## Oral Privacy & Conversations

Conversations regarding patients must be kept strictly to confidential, work-related environments.

**Never discuss patient details or work situations in:**

- Elevators, stairwells, or hallways
- Cafeterias, breakrooms, or outdoor smoking areas
- Public transport or off-site venues
- Social media accounts (public or private)

**Note:** Removing a patient's name does not make a conversation compliant. Descriptive details (e.g., unique condition, timing, location) can easily lead to identification.

## Handling Paper Records

Physical paper containing PHI must be protected throughout its lifecycle (45 CFR § 164.530(c)).

**Always:**

- Store physical files in locked cabinets or secure medical record rooms when not in immediate use.
- Dispose of paper PHI exclusively in designated, locked shredding bins.
- Use formal cover sheets when faxing documents if required by facility policy.

**Never:**

- Leave printed schedules, labels, or records on open printers or unattended desks.
- Throw paper containing PHI into standard trash or recycling cans.
- Remove physical paper records from the facility without explicit administrative authorization.

## Remote Work Expectations

Staff working remotely or in work-from-home settings are held to the same HIPAA security and privacy standards as on-site personnel.

**Always:**

- Connect exclusively through organization-approved Virtual Private Networks (VPN) and secure devices.
- Work in a private space where family members, visitors, or roommates cannot view screens or overhear phone conversations.
- Secure physical documents in locked storage; do not print at home unless specifically authorized.

**Never:**

- Work on PHI in public spaces (cafes, libraries, public Wi-Fi networks).
- Allow family members or unauthorized individuals to use work-issued devices.

## Social Media and Digital Footprint

Strict zero-tolerance policy regarding social media and patient privacy.

**Never post or share:**

- Any patient names, images, or identifying commentary.
- Photographs taken in patient care or administrative areas that capture screens, schedules, or records.
- Screenshots of electronic health record (EHR) systems.
- Anecdotes or stories regarding work events that describe patient scenarios, even if names are omitted.

## Incident Reporting & Escalation

Under the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule (45 CFR §§ 164.400–414), organizations must evaluate and report privacy breaches promptly.

### Immediate Action Required

Report suspected privacy or security incidents immediately to your supervisor or the designated Privacy/Compliance Officer. Examples to report include:

- Misdirected emails, faxes, or mailings containing PHI
- Lost or stolen work laptops, encrypted drives, or mobile devices
- Viewing of unauthorized records or suspected system compromise
- Accidental verbal disclosures to the wrong individual

Prompt internal reporting enables the organization to mitigate patient risk and comply with federal reporting timelines.

## Examples of HIPAA Violations

- Looking up a friend, family member, neighbor, or celebrity out of curiosity.
- Discussing patient details with unauthorized coworkers during break times.
- Sending a file containing patient data to an incorrect email recipient.
- Leaving detailed clinical information on an unverified voicemail system.
- Posting work experiences or screenshots on personal social media pages.
- Sharing login credentials or using a colleague's logged-in workstation.

## Consequences of Non-Compliance

Violations of HIPAA regulations or internal privacy policies lead to immediate investigation and corrective action:

- Mandatory compliance retraining
- Formal disciplinary warnings and personnel file documentation
- Revocation of system access permissions
- Termination of employment
- Reporting to professional licensing boards
- Civil and criminal penalties enforced by federal agencies (including fines and potential imprisonment for willful violations)

## Daily Checklist for Frontline Staff

- **Minimum Necessary:** Am I accessing only the specific data needed for my current task?
- **Verification:** Have I prompted the caller to state identifiers before revealing information?
- **Screen Safeguards:** Is my screen locked when I step away?
- **Physical Security:** Are paper documents hidden from public view and placed in shred bins when finished?
- **Oral Privacy:** Am I speaking quietly and avoiding patient discussions in public zones?
- **Communication:** Am I using secure, approved systems to transmit data?

## Questions & Escalation Path

When uncertain about whether information may be accessed, used, or shared:

1. Pause the action or disclosure.
2. Consult internal written Standard Operating Procedures (SOPs).
3. Escalate to your direct supervisor, the Privacy Officer, or the Compliance Department before proceeding.
